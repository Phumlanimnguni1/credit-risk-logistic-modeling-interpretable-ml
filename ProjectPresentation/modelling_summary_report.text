Exploratory Data Analysis Findings Using our interactive EDA tool to guide our modelling choices, 
we systematically evaluated the predictive power of all variables in the dataset.

Our analysis revealed that the strongest predictors of default risk are financial metrics, specifically annual_income and interest_rate.

We identified medium predictive power in a mix of demographic and historical credit features: 
age, employment_length_years, num_delinquencies_2yr, and months_since_oldest_account.

The remaining five columns (loan_amount, dti_ratio, pct_accounts_current, months_since_last_delinquency, num_open_accounts) exhibited only weak predictive value.

Importantly, our univariate and bivariate explorations found a large subset of features to be useless predictors of credit risk. 

These non-predictive features include home_ownership, region, total_revolving_balance, credit_utilisation_pct, num_hard_inquiries_6mo, loan_purpose, application_dow, branch_code_id, months_at_current_address, email_domain_type, phone_verified, and the set data split identifier.

Feature Engineering and Regulatory Compliance The business specifically requires an interpretable logistic regression model that can be clearly explained to regulators and risk managers.
As part of our research into features that a regulator might disapprove of,
we applied strict fair-lending principles to our feature selection:

Geographic Redlining Avoidance: Geographic features like region and branch_code_id can act as proxies for historical wealth disparities and lead to discriminatory redlining. 

Fortunately, our EDA proved these features are useless predictors in this specific dataset.
 
We completely dropped them from our model, ensuring ethical compliance without sacrificing performance.

Demographic Compliance (Ageism): 

While our EDA showed that age is a medium predictor of default risk, age is a protected demographic class.

Regulators require lenders to evaluate applicants based on financial behavior rather than age.
To guarantee strict regulatory compliance, we intentionally chose to drop age from our final feature set.
By eliminating useless variables to reduce noise, and deliberately dropping non-compliant demographic features, our final logistic regression model relies strictly on permissible and highly interpretable metrics like income and interest rates.

Feature Engineering:

- 1. annual_income (Strong) vs loan_amount (Weak)
What to explore: Plot income against the loan amount, colored by your default_flag. 
Even though loan_amount is a weak predictor on its own, comparing it to income reveals the applicant's true capacity to borrow.
The Feature to Create: Loan-to-Income Ratio (loan_amount / annual_income). 
This explicit mathematical feature will tell your model if someone is dangerously over-borrowing relative to their strong or weak salary.

- 2. interest_rate (Strong) vs dti_ratio (Weak)
What to explore: High debt-to-income (dti_ratio) might only be a massive default risk when the cost of borrowing (interest_rate) is also painfully high. 
Look for a cluster of defaults where both numbers spike.
The Feature to Create: Debt Servicing Stress Index (interest_rate * dti_ratio). 
By multiplying these together, you force the logistic regression model to apply a heavy penalty to applicants who are both highly indebted and facing expensive interest payments.

- 3. months_since_oldest_account (Medium) vs num_delinquencies_2yr (Medium)
What to explore: This pair uncovers the difference between a "chronic defaulter" and a "once-off mistake".
One delinquency on a 2-year-old credit profile is a terrible sign, but one delinquency on a 20-year-old credit profile might be statistically harmless.
The Feature to Create: Delinquency Concentration (num_delinquencies_2yr / months_since_oldest_account). 
This normalises their bad behavior over their total credit lifespan, giving the model a much more accurate picture of their historical reliability.

- 4. annual_income (Strong) vs employment_length_years (Medium)
What to explore: Plot these to look for "Income Stability".
 A person earning $150,000 but who has only been employed for 0.5 years might actually default more often than someone earning $50,000 who has been at their job for 10 years.
The Feature to Create: You could create a scorecard-style interaction
by binning this pair. For example, create a binary feature (1 or 0) for "High Earners with Low Stability" to explicitly flag this risky subgroup to the model.
